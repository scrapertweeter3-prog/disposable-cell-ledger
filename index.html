A $6 light buys itself in cells every eight months

A sealed LED puck light costs $6 and promises years of glow on a set of disposables. The honest spec sits in the fine print: three AAA cells at about 1,200 mAh each, an LED that draws around 30 mA. That is 3,600 mAh feeding a 30 mA draw, 120 hours of light per set. Put it on evening closet duty, two hours a day, and 730 hours a year runs through about six sets. One set lasts two months.

The ledger is where the promise breaks. A 24 pack of alkaline AAAs runs $12, fifty cents a cell, so a set is $1.50. Six sets a year is $9 in cells. A $6 fixture that consumes $9 a year repays its own sticker price in cells every eight months, and nobody shopping the aisle reads it that way. The light is not a purchase, it is a meter.

Run it for the life of the house. Twenty years at six sets a year is 120 sets, $180 in cells, thirty times the $6 sticker. The disposables for one puck light buy thirty more puck lights. The cells were never an accessory, they are the product, and the $6 was the enrollment fee.

The sealed body makes it worse. No way to reseat a contact, no way to clean a corroded terminal, a battery door latch that cracks years before the cells quit. When it fails you buy the whole fixture again, on the light's schedule instead of yours. Disposables stack badly by the drawer too: ten puck lights on alkalines burn $1,800 in cells over twenty years and all ten go dark the week the spare pack runs out.

Rechargeables break the meter. A set of NiMH AAAs costs about $7.50 and survives around 500 recharges, and at two months a charge that is a thousand months of light, 83 years. One rechargeable set outlives the fixture it powers. The sealed light is priced like a trivial buy and wired like a subscription. Cost per decade is the only way to see it: $30 a decade for the disposable version, $7.50 once for the one that opens.
